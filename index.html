The big picture: 1 vote, 3 markets

The US midterm result pushed oil down, pulled foreign money into India and knocked the AI trade, all within 2 days.

In short
  • Democrats won both houses of the US Congress, and voters said the Iran war was the main reason.
  • Markets read that as a sign the war may wind down, so oil fell and India's shares jumped.
  • The same vote hurt the AI and data-centre trade, so chip-heavy markets like Korea fell hard.

3 big stories ran together this time: a US election, a crash in oil, and record foreign buying in India. They are a single story. The map below shows where each force stands for an Indian investor.

The pattern is unusual. Many world tiles are red because rates are rising in Europe and diesel is scarce. But the tiles that matter most to India have turned green at once: cheaper oil, a stronger rupee, falling bond yields and foreign money coming back.

Why a US vote moved Indian shares

Democrats took the House and the Senate together, the first such double flip since 2006. The Vice President said plainly that "the reason we lost is the war". Traders took that as a signal that the US-Iran war has lost political support.

  • Oil — Brent fell from about $118 to near $93 in under a week, its biggest weekly drop since April 2020 (section 03).
  • India — the Nifty rose 576 points on 5 November, its biggest one-day rally in 7 months (section 04).
  • AI trade — the vote went against new data centres, and chip and memory shares fell for 2 days (section 09).
  • Rates — US bond yields slipped below 5%, but Europe kept raising rates (section 02).

Reading between the lines, India gained from both halves of the vote. It imports most of its oil, so a lower price helps. And it has almost no AI hardware exports, so money leaving the AI trade had a natural place to go.

Is this a turn or a bounce?

Views split sharply. One view is that India may be entering a new bull market: oil is down, the rupee is steady, foreign funds are back, tax collections are up 23% and company profits are growing at their fastest pace in years. The opposite view is that the mood has swung too fast. The same shares that nobody wanted 2 weeks ago are now being bought at high prices, and the war is not over.

  • For the bulls — the Nifty traded at about 17 times next year's profit at its low, and its 5-year return had fallen to about 5% a year.
  • For the bears — domestic funds sold a record ₹10,445 crore on the same day foreigners bought, and US officials are still preparing for possible new strikes on Iran.
  • The test — whether oil stays below $100 and whether foreign buying lasts beyond short-covering (closing out old bets on a fall).

So the rally rests on a single fragile thing: the war staying quiet. Every other green tile depends on it.

The price of money: India eases, the West tightens

Indian borrowing costs fell fast as oil dropped, while Britain, Canada and Sweden raised rates and the US weighs a hike.

In short
  • India's 10-year bond yield fell sharply in 10 days as oil and US yields came down.
  • Central banks in Britain, Canada and Sweden raised rates because inflation is still high.
  • Gold jumped again, and central banks are buying it at the fastest pace since 2024.

India's bond yield turned down

A bond yield is the yearly return a lender asks for to lend money for that long. India's long-term yield had climbed to a 4-year high in late October. It then dropped about 40 basis points, or 0.4 of a percentage point.

Low of 6.685% on 6 July, August level about 6.75%, high of 7.483% on 26 October, then 7.192% and 7.09%.

3 things drove the fall: cheaper oil, lower US yields, and traders closing bets on higher yields. A lower yield cuts the cost of borrowing for the government, banks and companies.

One view is that Indian bonds still carry a 7-year-high risk premium (the extra return over US bonds), so there is room for yields to fall further. The opposite view is that the premium is high for a reason: the rupee lost about 8% between January and May, and a weak monsoon could lift food prices.

The West is still raising rates

The Bank of England raised its rate for the first time since 2023, by 8 votes to 1. Its governor said it will not be the last hike unless the Middle East war ends. Euro-area inflation hit twice the target.

  • UK — the bank now sees inflation peaking at 4.9% in mid-2027; the 10-year gilt yields about 5.15%.
  • US — odds of a December Fed hike rose after strong services data; jobless claims hit a 57-year low.
  • US bonds — the 10-year yield fell below 5% after the vote, to about 4.83%.
  • China — the central bank raised the share of foreign currency banks must hold in reserve, to slow a fast-rising yuan.

Views split on how far hikes must go. One view is that true US inflation is only about 2.2%, pushed up by AI equipment and fuel, so markets are pricing too many hikes. The opposite view points to US producer prices rising about 6% a year, far faster than shop prices, which means more price rises are still coming.

Who funds the US deficit

The US government kept its bond sales unchanged for an 11th quarter but dropped the words "at least" from its guidance. That hints at larger long-bond sales later. US debt passed $40 trillion, rising about $10 billion a day.

So more of the deficit will be funded with short bills. AI companies are also borrowing heavily (section 09), which adds to the supply of bonds the market must take.

Gold and the rupee

  • Gold — rose 3% on 4 November and above $4,100 the next day, after briefly dipping below $4,000 on margin-call selling.
  • Central banks — bought 321 tonnes in the third quarter, 42% more than a year ago, led by China and Poland.
  • Coins and bars — demand fell 19%, so ordinary buyers are stepping back at these prices.
  • Rupee — near ₹94.6 a dollar, close to the strong end of its range since May.

Reading between the lines, the gold buyers now are governments, not households. That makes gold less sensitive to Indian wedding and festival demand and more tied to how much countries trust the dollar.

Indian lenders still pay more

Lower government yields have not yet reached smaller lenders. Several finance companies said their own borrowing costs are still rising.

03

Oil, ships and war: the price fell, the danger did not

Crude lost about a fifth of its price on hopes of peace, but tankers are still being hit and diesel is still scarce.

In short
  • Oil crashed because traders bet the US vote will cool the Iran war.
  • But ships are still being attacked near Hormuz, and fuel stocks are very low.
  • Iran said Indian ships can pass freely, which helps India if it holds.

How fast oil fell

US crude lost about $16 a barrel in 3 sessions, the steepest such drop since April 2020. Energy shares had their worst day of the year.

Brent crude, $ a barrel

Prices as reported in market notes; a fall of about $20 in a week.

Part of the fall was the vote, and part was supply. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iraq and Kuwait shipped over 15 million barrels a day in October, up 10%. A key physical price in the North Sea swung from $20 above the benchmark to below it.

Reading between the lines, the "fear premium" is draining out. In a physical market, buyers had been paying extra to get barrels at all. That extra has now almost gone.

Is the war really ending?

Views split hard here. One view is that the market is pricing a peace that does not exist. Iran wants permanent control of the strait, the US president no longer faces an election, and US news reports say the military is preparing for possible "major" combat. The opposite view is that the vote was a referendum on the war, so the Iran trade is reversing.

  • Attacks — Iran's Guards said 4 tankers hit mines south of Hormuz; October had over 35 shipping incidents, the most since the war began.
  • Red Sea — the Houthis seized ground near Bab al-Mandeb and asked Saudi Arabia for $51 billion to stop.
  • Talks — Qatar-led US-Iran talks ran 3 days with no breakthrough.
  • Washington — the president said there are no immediate plans to strike, but forces are "locked and loaded".
  • History — after losing the 2006 midterms, the US fired its defence chief and then sent more troops 3 months later. Blame does not always mean retreat.

What it means for India

Iran said ships from India, China, Iraq and Qatar may pass the strait without its restrictions. India was not charged a transit fee, though Chinese ships reportedly were. The claim came from an Iranian lawmaker and has not been confirmed by India.

  • Refiners — Indian refiners bought more Middle East crude instead of Russian oil, because shipping Russian barrels now costs more.
  • Sanctions — separately, the US sanctioned a Chandigarh couple and several Indian firms for running fake ship registries for Iran.
  • Companies hit — KEC International cut its growth outlook on West Asia; Devson Catalyst's exports fell from 43% to 7% of sales.
  • Gas sellers — Indraprastha Gas's profit fell 94% and Mahanagar Gas's 29% on costly spot gas.
  • Ports — Adani Ports agreed to buy Evos, a European fuel-storage firm, for €2.56 billion.

Reading between the lines, India sits on both sides of the war. It gets favoured passage from Iran, yet its firms are caught by US sanctions. That balance can break quickly if fighting resumes.

India's market: foreigners buy, locals sell

After 16 months of selling, foreign funds bought at a record pace, while Indian funds sold into the rally.

In short
  • Foreign investors bought more Indian shares in 1 day than at any time in about 5.5 years.
  • Indian mutual funds and insurers sold heavily on the same day.
  • Much of the foreign buying was closing old bets on a fall, not fresh long-term money yet.

The flow turned

Foreign investors (FIIs) sold Indian shares for 16 straight months. So far in November they have bought ₹32,210 crore, the best month since late 2023.

The tax question

The Finance Minister said she will look at concerns over capital gains tax. Brokers and exchange shares jumped on the hope.

  • The ask — industry body FICCI wants the tax exemption on listed shares restored and a stable 10-year regime.
  • Against — Prashant Jain of 3P Capital says a cut is not needed, since salaried people earning ₹30-50 lakh pay 30%.
  • Why foreigners left — a lawyer argues foreigners left over profits, prices and the rupee, not a 12.5% tax, so a cut would cost revenue for little gain.

Reading between the lines, the rally did not need the tax cut. Oil and short-covering did the work. A cut would be a bonus, and its absence in the Budget could disappoint traders who are now pricing it in.

Exchanges, listings and a warning

  • Exchanges — BSE's profit rose 45% and MCX's more than doubled; brokerages started covering the newly listed NSE with targets of ₹2,050-2,200.
  • Listings — Jio Platforms listed at a ₹13.5 lakh crore value, and the discount on its parent Reliance narrowed from about 30% to 20-25%.
  • Coming up — Razorpay won approval for a share sale of about ₹5,500-6,500 crore, at about $5 billion, well below its $7.5 billion peak.
  • Stockal — about 30 Indian investors say their US shares (Apple, Nvidia, Amazon) were sold without consent and the money put into a tiny Chinese stock that fell about 60%.
  • Funds — several flexi-cap funds sold TCS and trimmed Infosys while adding Lenskart and Eternal.

The Stockal case shows a gap in overseas investing. Shares bought through a GIFT City platform still sit with a US partner, which India's regulator cannot fully supervise.

India's economy: strong demand, a dry monsoon

Services, credit and tax collections are running hot, but rain, input costs and US tariffs are pulling the other way.

In short
  • Service businesses grew at a 4-month high while their costs rose the least in 6 years.
  • The Cabinet approved big schemes for power storage and battery minerals.
  • A weak monsoon and costlier milk, rubber and steel are the main worries.

Demand is strong

06

Power, storage and orders: the build-out keeps going

A big storage scheme, a ₹20,000 crore-plus power order and a flood of new contracts, even as AI-linked equipment shares fell.

In short
  • India will fund 92 GWh of power storage, mostly pumped hydro, with a local-content rule for batteries.
  • Order books at power, cable and engineering firms keep hitting records.
  • But Indian shares tied to the US AI boom fell hard after the US vote.

The storage scheme

Storage lets the grid save solar power for the evening. The new scheme adds 92 GWh, which is 170% of what India has today. Battery projects must use at least 40% Indian content.

Why voters turned on data centres

Local ballot measures to restrict data centres passed 43 to 1. Texas elected a governor who promised a pause on new data-centre permits, and Texas holds about a quarter of planned US capacity.

  • Opinion — the share of Americans who strongly oppose a data centre near them rose from 24% to 61% in under a year.
  • OpenAI — pledged to pay the full power cost of its own sites and to stop asking officials to sign secrecy deals.

Views split. One view is that this is a real brake: power bills have jumped, and Democrats now control the purse. The opposite view, from JPMorgan's credit team, is that 2027 spending will barely change, because counties that voted for limits hold under 5% of the sites already being built.
